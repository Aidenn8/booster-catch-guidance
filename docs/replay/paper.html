1Introduction

Super Heavy boosters return to the launch site and are caught by a pair of arms on the launch tower instead of landing on legs. The last seconds of the landing burn are therefore a precision-guidance problem with unusual structure: the target is a point in the air beside a 146 m steel structure, the engine count drops from 13 to 3 partway through the burn, and near the end the booster is so light that three engines at minimum throttle almost exactly support it.

Powered-descent guidance by convex optimization is well established. Lossless convexification turns the non-convex thrust bounds of the soft-landing problem into a second-order cone program whose solution is optimal for the original problem [1], [2], and convex optimization has been reported in the landing guidance of reusable rockets [3]. This study asks what such guidance buys for a tower catch compared with a well-engineered heuristic, and where its weak points are. Our contributions are:

  • A three-degree-of-freedom simulation of the terminal phase with the tower geometry, the 13 → 3 engine switch, engine lag and a Monte Carlo dispersion model, in which the two controllers share sensing, estimation, terminal descent and abort logic so that only the guidance law differs.
  • A convex model-predictive controller with tower-specific constraints (an offshore half-space, a glide-slope cone, an optional approach-speed cap and an optional engine-out braking reserve), verified against independent solutions.
  • Campaigns on identical dispersed flights quantifying catch rate, precision, propellant margin, crosswind limits, the capture basin and the propellant cost of engine-out tolerance.
  • Seven failure mechanisms found by tracing individual closed-loop flights (§6).

2Problem setup

The frame has x pointing from offshore towards the tower and z up. The landing burn begins 1,250 m above the ground, 280 m offshore, falling at 270 m/s. Vehicle and scenario parameters are public estimates [7] (Table 1).

Outcomes. A flight is caught if the centre of mass passes the catch height within tolerance. A booster that misses keeps flying, diverting offshore, until it reaches the ground; this is a safe miss if it comes down more than 40 m from the tower and a pad strike otherwise. Contact with the tower structure is always a pad strike.

The throttle floor. Three engines at minimum throttle produce \(3 \times 0.4 \times 2.256 = 2.71\) MN, while a near-empty booster weighs about 2.75 MN. Near the end of the burn the booster can therefore brake at up to about 13 m/s² but can accelerate its descent by only a few tenths of a m/s². Over-braking close to the arms is unrecoverable (an early version stalled 12 m above the arms and hovered until its propellant ran out), and a constant-speed final descent would sit on the floor with no authority to correct downwards. Both controllers therefore hand over at the terminal gate to the same law, a constant 1 m/s² deceleration from 5 to 1 m/s, which keeps thrust about 10% above the floor.

3Guidance

3.1 Convex model-predictive control

Following [1], [2], with \(\mathbf u = \mathbf T/m\), slack \(\sigma = \Gamma/m\) and \(z = \ln m\), the controller solves, from the current estimated state to the terminal gate,

\[ \begin{aligned} \underset{\mathbf u,\,\sigma}{\text{maximize}}\quad & z(t_f) \\ \text{subject to}\quad & \dot{\mathbf r} = \mathbf v, \qquad \dot{\mathbf v} = \mathbf u + \mathbf g + \hat{\mathbf a}(t), \qquad \dot z = -\alpha\,\sigma, \\ & \lVert \mathbf u \rVert \le \sigma, \qquad u_z \ge \sigma\cos\theta_{\max}(t), \\ & \mu_{\min}(t)\bigl[1 - \delta z + \tfrac12\,\delta z^{2}\bigr] \le \sigma \le \mu_{\max}(t)\bigl[1 - \delta z\bigr], \\ & \tan\gamma\,\lVert \mathbf r_{xy} - \mathbf r^{\star}_{xy} \rVert \le r_z - r^{\star}_z, \\ & \mathbf r(t_f) = \mathbf r_{\mathrm g}, \quad \mathbf v(t_f) = (0,\,0,\,-v_{\mathrm g}), \quad z(t_f) \ge \ln\bigl(m_{\mathrm{dry}} + m_{\mathrm{td}}\bigr), \end{aligned} \tag{1} \]

where \(\delta z = z - z_0(t)\) with \(z_0(t)\) the logarithm of the lightest reachable mass, \(\mu_{\min,\max}(t) = n(t)\,T_{\min,\max}\,e^{-z_0(t)}\) for \(n(t)\) live engines, \(\alpha = 1/(I_{\mathrm{sp}} g_0)\), \(\mathbf r^{\star}\) is the catch point, \(\gamma = 20^\circ\) and \(m_{\mathrm{td}} \approx 3.9\) t is the propellant the terminal descent will need. The disturbance \(\hat{\mathbf a}\) combines drag, evaluated on the previous plan's velocities and refined in a second pass, with the observer's estimate (§3.3). The upper thrust bound carries an 8% margin and the tilt limit \(\theta_{\max}\) is 13° (15° less a 2° margin), tightening to 3° in the 2 s before the gate. The relaxation is lossless (\(\lVert\mathbf u\rVert = \sigma\) at the optimum); plans that would command less than minimum throttle are rejected. Before the commit window (6 s before the gate) the trajectory is confined to a half-space offshore of the tower.

Two optional convex constraints are evaluated as variants. The approach cap limits the closing speed towards the tower to 10 m/s inside the commit window. The engine-out braking reserve requires, throughout the 3-engine phase, that the remaining engines could still bring the booster to the gate speed if one failed:

\[ v_z^{2} \le v_{\mathrm g}^{2} + 2\,a_{\mathrm{eo}}\,\bigl(r_z - h_{\mathrm g}\bigr), \qquad a_{\mathrm{eo}} = \frac{(n-1)\,T^{\mathrm{eng}}_{\max}}{m_0} - g_0 , \tag{2} \]

a square bounded by an affine function, hence a second-order cone constraint.

Problem (1) is discretized on 32 nodes with a node on every rule boundary (engine switch, commit window, final alignment) and solved with Clarabel [5] through CVXPY [4] in 0.1–0.3 s. Because every extra second of flight is gravity loss, propellant use rises monotonically with \(t_f\) inside the feasible window, and the controller takes the earliest feasible \(t_f\), found by bisection on feasibility. It re-plans 0.5 s after ignition and then every second, freezing the plan 2.5 s before the gate, and tracks the plan with a PD correction. If no plan exists, the margins are relaxed in tiers and a faster gate arrival (up to 12 m/s) is admitted; if the problem is still infeasible more than 3 s before the gate, infeasibility is taken as a certificate that the catch is lost and the booster aborts. An engine failure triggers an immediate re-plan with the reduced engine count.

3.2 Braking-profile baseline

Vertically, the baseline applies during the 13-engine phase the constant acceleration that places the booster on a constant-deceleration speed profile at the engine switch, then follows that profile (deceleration \(a_2\), tuned) to the gate. Laterally it follows a braking profile (3 m/s², capped at 45 m/s) to a holding point offshore of the tower, retargeting the catch point when the commit window opens. On an engine failure it rescales \(a_2\) to the remaining braking capability, and it aborts if the braking needed to reach the gate exceeds 95% of what the engines can supply.

3.3 Shared components

To isolate the guidance law, both controllers use the same thrust model (commands pass through the nominal engine limits and a 0.25 s first-order lag) and mass estimate; the same observer, which attributes unexplained acceleration along the thrust to a thrust-effectiveness factor \(k\) (thrust bias and mass error) and across it to a residual (mostly wind), scaling commands by \(1/k\) and feeding the residual forward; the same terminal descent, entered one lag early; and the same go/no-go check at the gate (predicted lateral stopping point within 1.5 m), tower keep-out monitor and offshore divert of up to 30° tilt after an abort.

3.4 Verification

The guidance and simulator were checked against independent solutions (Table 2). At a fixed flight time the propellant is nearly independent of the thrust profile, because the boundary conditions fix the vertical impulse; the discriminating test is therefore the free-final-time comparison with SLSQP on the original non-convex problem.

4Experimental setup

The simulator integrates a point mass with RK4 at 50 Hz; the thrust direction stands in for attitude. Drag uses a crossflow model: vertical flow on the 64 m² base and horizontal flow on the 639 m² side. Monte Carlo dispersions are listed in Table 3. A 15 m/s crosswind pushes the booster sideways at 0.22 m/s², while the terminal descent's tilt limit allows only about 0.5 m/s² of correction, so wind is a real constraint here.

Protocol. Controller settings were chosen on a separate tuning set (40 dispersed flights, seed 99) by a rule fixed in advance: highest catch rate, ties broken by propellant remaining. This selected an 8% thrust margin and 2° tilt margin for the MPC and \(a_2 = 8\) m/s² for the baseline. Evaluation used fresh draws: a 300-flight Monte Carlo (seed 2026), a sweep of engine-failure times, a crosswind sweep, a grid of ignition points and a propellant study. Every controller flew the same flights in every campaign. Intervals are 95% Wilson score intervals [6].

5Results

5.1 Nominal flight

In calm air both controllers catch the booster (Figure 2). The MPC arrives 1 cm from the catch point with 5.5 t of propellant after 17.1 s; the baseline arrives 45 cm off with 3.9 t after 18.9 s. The MPC's plan brakes late and hard, which is where its propellant saving comes from and, as §5.4 shows, where its vulnerability comes from.

5.2 Propellant versus robustness

The baseline's single parameter trades one quantity for the other: brake gently and it lands with 2 t; brake hard and it has propellant to spare but stops passing the go/no-go check at the gate (45% caught at \(a_2 = 10\)). Every MPC setting lies above and to the right of the baseline's curve (Figure 3).

5.3 Dispersed Monte Carlo

Over 300 dispersed flights, 56 of which drew an engine failure, the tuned MPC and the capped MPC each caught 86.3% against the baseline's 73.0% (Table 4). Without an engine failure the MPC is nearly perfect, 243–244 of 244 against 217 (Table 5); the baseline's misses are aborts after a low or slow start (46 of its 81 misses) or a no-go at the gate (29). The MPC also lands 9× more precisely: median distance from the catch point 4.2 cm (95th percentile 9.5 cm) against 38 cm (76 cm), and 95th-percentile lateral speed 0.08 against 0.31 m/s (Figure 5).

Engine failures separate the variants. The tuned MPC saves 16 of 56 boosters to the baseline's 2, but 20 of its aborts come down near the pad, because its late-braking profile leaves less room to divert. The approach cap converts 15 of those pad strikes into safe misses at an unchanged catch rate, for 1.5 t of propellant. The tuning rule did not anticipate this: on a tuning set with three engine failures the pad-strike difference was 1 against 0. A rule that weights pad safety, the outcome that matters most for a tower catch, selects the capped MPC. The engine-out reserve does not survive full dispersions with 45 t: with about 1 t of slack it either declines catches it cannot afford (129 safe aborts) or runs dry in the terminal descent (21), since a 2% thrust bias moves its propellant estimate by about 1 t (§5.7).

5.4 Engine-failure timing

One engine fails at a single moment between ignition and 16 s, in calm air with the nominal vehicle (Table 6, Figure 6). The tuned MPC saves the booster when the failure comes early, when 13 engines can absorb it, or after about 11.5 s, when little braking remains. In between it correctly finds that no catch exists, but its aborts come down near the pad in 13 of 33 cases: braking late, it is lower and faster than the baseline at the same moment (72 m lower at 6 s) and has less time to steer clear. The baseline brakes earlier, so its aborts usually clear the pad, but it rarely saves the booster. With the engine-out reserve the booster survives a failure at any time except 1.0 and 2.0 s (both safe misses). It does so by braking harder while 13 engines are available and descending more slowly on three, at a cost of about 5 t of propellant: it lands with 0.5 t instead of 5.5 t.

5.5 Crosswind

Both controllers fail between 20 and 25 m/s of mean wind (Table 7, Figure 7), the MPC later than the baseline. At 25 m/s the wind pushes the 71 m booster sideways at about 0.6 m/s² plus gusts, more than the terminal descent's 2.7° tilt can correct (about 0.5 m/s²). That limit is set by the catch geometry, not by either guidance law.

5.6 Capture basin

Shifting the ignition point by up to ±200 m towards or away from the tower and ±300 m in altitude (143 points, calm air), the MPC catches 127 with no pad strikes; the baseline catches 86 with 25 pad strikes (Figure 8). The fixed braking profile cannot absorb a low or far-offshore start and discovers the problem at the gate, where an abort is too late to clear the pad. The MPC re-plans from wherever it is, and where no catch exists its planner reports this seconds earlier, including when the remaining propellant could not cover the terminal descent.

5.7 The propellant cost of engine-out tolerance

The same 40 dispersed flights (20 with an engine failure at a random time) were flown with 45, 50 and 55 t of landing propellant (Table 8, Figure 9). About 5 t more (+11%) makes single-engine-out tolerance robust to the dispersions: 19 of 20 failures survived with no pad strikes (95% interval roughly 76–99%). Extra propellant alone does nothing for the tuned MPC, which catches 4 of 20 at every level. It is the braking-reserve constraint that converts propellant into engine-out safety, by spending it on braking while 13 engines are available.

6Failure mechanisms found in closed loop

Each of the following was a failure in an earlier version, found by tracing individual flights. They are properties of the problem rather than incidental bugs, and they are likely the most transferable part of the study.

  1. Fuel-optimal plans ride every limit at once. The optimum brakes as late as possible at full thrust and full tilt. A thrust margin alone left the plan on the tilt limit, where any lateral error made it infeasible; margins on both (8%, 2°) were needed.
  2. Actuator lag against bang-bang plans. The engines' 0.25 s response to a max–min–max thrust plan lost 1–3 m/s of braking at every switch, always in the dangerous direction. Commanding the plan one lag early, modelling the engine-switch tail-off and entering the terminal descent one lag early removed the loss.
  3. The throttle floor makes over-braking a trap. Clipping an oversized lateral command by rotating the thrust vector turned the excess into braking and stalled the booster 12 m above the arms. The lateral component must be trimmed instead.
  4. The estimator must match what the planner needs. Explaining a thrust shortfall as extra mass reproduces the accelerations but corrupts the planner's propellant budget by about 10 t, producing false declarations that the catch was impossible. The planner now receives the engine thrust scaled by the estimated effectiveness.
  5. A planner must agree with itself. Re-planning from a point on its own plan, under the same rules, was infeasible: drag entered by successive passes and the first pass ignored it, although drag supplies up to 8 m/s² of braking. This one consistency check removed most of the closed-loop propellant loss (about 2 t) and most false aborts.
  6. Constraints checked at nodes leak between them. Time-indexed rules checked only at grid points let plans cross between points; the next plan, on a different grid, found the same timing infeasible and slipped its arrival (about 0.5 t per half second). A node on every rule boundary fixed this.
  7. Infeasibility is a useful certificate only if the model is honest. Aborting when the planner finds no solution decides seconds earlier than a heuristic could, but every modelling gap above first appeared as a false abort. The plan must also budget the propellant for the phase it does not plan, the terminal descent, or it will succeed on paper and run dry over the arms.

7Limitations

  • Point mass. The thrust direction stands in for attitude: there are no rotational dynamics, gimbal limits, grid fins, slosh, flex or aerodynamic moments. The tower-strike check uses the centre of mass plus the booster radius, consistent with a body held near upright while the engines gimbal. The replay in Figure 1 draws the body leaning about a third of the way into the thrust, with the engine plumes deflected for the remainder; this is a rendering choice, not simulated attitude.
  • Public estimates. Masses, propellant, drag coefficients, tower geometry and tolerances are estimates. Absolute catch rates should not be read as predictions for the real vehicle; the comparisons, mechanisms and trade-offs are the result.
  • Environment. Constant air density and a uniform mean wind with Ornstein–Uhlenbeck gusts; no shear or tower wake.
  • Engines. A single total thrust with first-order lag. A real engine failure would add a thrust transient and a moment, and detection is assumed to be instantaneous.
  • Computation. 0.1–0.3 s per plan in Python; flight software would use an embedded solver.
  • Sample sizes. 40 flights per tuning setting and 12 per wind speed; intervals are wide and are reported.

8Conclusion

In this model, convex fuel-optimal guidance outperforms a tuned braking heuristic on every nominal measure of a tower catch: catch rate, precision, propellant margin, crosswind tolerance and capture basin. The property that saves propellant, braking late, also leaves less room to abort after a mid-burn engine failure, which a 10 m/s approach cap largely restores at no cost in catch rate. Single-engine-out tolerance is available as one additional convex constraint plus about 5 t of landing propellant. The throttle floor, rather than the guidance law, shapes the final seconds of the catch.
