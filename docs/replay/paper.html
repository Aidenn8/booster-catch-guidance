Background

Super Heavy boosters return to the launch site and are caught by a pair of arms on the launch tower instead of landing on legs. The last seconds of the landing burn are a precision-guidance problem with an unusual structure: the target is a point in the air beside a 146 m steel tower, the engine count drops from 13 to 3 partway through the burn, and near the end the booster is so light that three engines at minimum throttle almost exactly support its weight.

Convex optimization is an established tool for powered-descent guidance. Lossless convexification turns the non-convex thrust limits of a soft landing into a second-order cone program whose solution is optimal for the original problem [1][2], and convex solvers are reported in the landing guidance of reusable rockets [3]. This study asks what that kind of guidance buys for a tower catch compared with a well-engineered heuristic, and where its weak points are.

  • A three-degree-of-freedom simulation of the terminal phase with the tower geometry, the 13 → 3 engine switch, engine lag and a Monte Carlo dispersion model. Both controllers share sensing, estimation, terminal descent and abort logic, so only the guidance law differs.
  • A convex model-predictive controller with tower-specific constraints, verified against independent solutions.
  • Campaigns on identical dispersed flights measuring catch rate, precision, propellant margin, crosswind limits, the capture basin and the propellant cost of engine-out tolerance.
  • Seven failure mechanisms found by tracing individual closed-loop flights.

Setup

The landing burn begins 1,250 m above the ground and 280 m offshore, with the booster falling at 270 m/s. Distances are measured towards the tower, with altitude up. Vehicle figures are public estimates [7].

Outcomes. A flight is caught if the centre of mass passes the catch height within tolerance. A booster that misses keeps flying, diverting offshore, until it reaches the ground: a safe miss if it comes down more than 40 m from the tower, a pad strike otherwise. Contact with the tower is always a pad strike.

The throttle floor. Three engines at minimum throttle produce 2.71 MN, while a near-empty booster weighs about 2.75 MN. Late in the burn the booster can brake at up to 13 m/s² but can speed up its descent by only a few tenths of a m/s². Over-braking near the arms cannot be undone (an early version stalled 12 m above the arms and hovered until its propellant ran out), and a constant-speed final descent would sit on the floor with no authority to correct downwards. Both controllers therefore hand over at the terminal gate to the same law, a constant 1 m/s² deceleration from 5 to 1 m/s, which keeps thrust about 10% above the floor.

Guidance

Convex model-predictive control

Following [1][2], the planner works with thrust acceleration u = T/m, a slack σ on its magnitude and log-mass z = ln m. Each second it solves, from the current estimated state to the terminal gate:

\[ \begin{aligned} \underset{\mathbf u,\,\sigma}{\text{maximize}}\quad & z(t_f) \\ \text{subject to}\quad & \dot{\mathbf r} = \mathbf v, \qquad \dot{\mathbf v} = \mathbf u + \mathbf g + \hat{\mathbf a}(t), \qquad \dot z = -\alpha\,\sigma, \\ & \lVert \mathbf u \rVert \le \sigma, \qquad u_z \ge \sigma\cos\theta_{\max}(t), \\ & \mu_{\min}(t)\bigl[1 - \delta z + \tfrac12\,\delta z^{2}\bigr] \le \sigma \le \mu_{\max}(t)\bigl[1 - \delta z\bigr], \\ & \tan\gamma\,\lVert \mathbf r_{xy} - \mathbf r^{\star}_{xy} \rVert \le r_z - r^{\star}_z, \\ & \mathbf r(t_f) = \mathbf r_{\mathrm g}, \quad \mathbf v(t_f) = (0,\,0,\,-v_{\mathrm g}), \quad z(t_f) \ge \ln\bigl(m_{\mathrm{dry}} + m_{\mathrm{td}}\bigr). \end{aligned} \]

In words: keep as much mass as possible at the gate, subject to:

  • Thrust limits. The real limits are convexified about the lightest reachable mass, with an 8% margin on maximum thrust. The relaxation is lossless, and any plan that would command less than minimum throttle is rejected.
  • Tilt. At most 13° (15° less a 2° margin), tightening to 3° in the 2 s before the gate.
  • Approach geometry. A 20° glide-slope cone over the catch point, plus a half-space that keeps the booster offshore until the commit window opens 6 s before the gate.
  • Arrival. The plan must reach the gate centred at 5 m/s with about 3.9 t of propellant still on board for the terminal descent.
  • Disturbances. Drag, evaluated on the previous plan's velocities and refined in a second pass, plus the observer's estimate of the unexplained acceleration.

Two optional constraints are tested as variants. The approach cap limits closing speed towards the tower to 10 m/s inside the commit window. The engine-out braking reserve requires, throughout the 3-engine phase, that the engines left after one failure could still bring the booster to gate speed. It is a square bounded by an affine function, so the problem stays a cone program:

\[ v_z^{2} \le v_{\mathrm g}^{2} + 2\,a_{\mathrm{eo}}\,\bigl(r_z - h_{\mathrm g}\bigr), \qquad a_{\mathrm{eo}} = \frac{(n-1)\,T^{\mathrm{eng}}_{\max}}{m_0} - g_0 . \]

The problem is discretized on 32 nodes, with a node on every rule boundary, and solved with Clarabel [5] through CVXPY [4] in 0.1–0.3 s. Every extra second of flight is gravity loss, so propellant use rises with flight time inside the feasible window. The controller therefore takes the earliest feasible flight time, found by bisection on feasibility. It re-plans every second, freezes the plan 2.5 s before the gate, and tracks it with a PD correction. If no plan exists, it relaxes the margins in tiers and then admits a faster gate arrival. If the problem is still infeasible more than 3 s before the gate, it treats that as proof the catch is lost and aborts. An engine failure triggers an immediate re-plan with the reduced engine count.

Braking-profile baseline

Vertically, the baseline flies a constant-deceleration speed profile to the gate, with the deceleration a2 tuned. Laterally, it brakes at 3 m/s² (capped at 45 m/s) towards a holding point offshore of the tower, then retargets the catch point when the commit window opens. After an engine failure it rescales a2 to the remaining braking capability. It aborts if reaching the gate would need more than 95% of the braking the engines can supply.

Shared components

To isolate the guidance law, both controllers share the rest of the stack:

  • the thrust model (nominal limits and a 0.25 s engine lag) and the mass estimate;
  • an observer that attributes unexplained acceleration along the thrust to a thrust-effectiveness factor and across it to a residual, mostly wind;
  • the terminal descent, entered one lag early;
  • the go/no-go check at the gate, the tower keep-out monitor, and an offshore divert of up to 30° tilt after an abort.

Verification

The guidance and the simulator were checked against independent solutions. At a fixed flight time, propellant barely depends on the thrust profile, because the boundary conditions fix the vertical impulse. The free-final-time comparison against SLSQP on the original non-convex problem is therefore the decisive test.

Experiments

The simulator integrates a point mass with RK4 at 50 Hz, using the thrust direction in place of attitude. Drag uses a crossflow model on the 64 m² base and the 639 m² side. A 15 m/s crosswind pushes the booster sideways at 0.22 m/s², while the terminal descent's tilt limit allows only about 0.5 m/s² of correction, so wind is a real constraint.

Protocol. Settings were chosen on a separate tuning set of 40 dispersed flights by a rule fixed in advance: highest catch rate, ties broken by propellant remaining. The rule selected an 8% thrust margin and 2° tilt margin for the MPC, and a braking level of 8 m/s² for the baseline. Evaluation used fresh draws:

  • a 300-flight Monte Carlo;
  • a sweep of engine-failure times;
  • a crosswind sweep;
  • a grid of shifted ignition points;
  • a landing-propellant study.

Every controller flew the same flights in every campaign, and intervals are 95% Wilson score intervals [6].

Results

Nominal flight

In calm air both controllers catch the booster. The MPC arrives 1 cm from the catch point after 17.1 s with 5.5 t of propellant left. The baseline arrives 45 cm off after 18.9 s with 3.9 t left. The MPC's plan brakes late and hard, which is where its propellant saving comes from and, as the engine-failure results show, where its vulnerability comes from.

Propellant versus robustness

The baseline's single parameter trades one for the other. Braking gently, it lands with 2 t; braking hard, it keeps propellant but fails the go/no-go check at the gate more often (45% caught at 10 m/s²). Every MPC setting sits above and to the right of the baseline's curve.

300-flight Monte Carlo

Over 300 dispersed flights, 56 of which drew an engine failure, the MPC and the capped MPC each caught 86.3% against the baseline's 73.0%. Without an engine failure the MPC is nearly perfect, at 243–244 of 244 against 217. The baseline's misses are aborts after a low or slow start, or a no-go at the gate. The MPC is also nine times more precise: its median distance from the catch point is 4.2 cm against 38 cm, and its 95th-percentile lateral speed is 0.08 against 0.31 m/s.

Engine-failure timing

Here one engine fails at a single moment between ignition and 16 s, in calm air with the nominal vehicle. The tuned MPC saves the booster when the failure comes early, while 13 engines can absorb it, or after about 11.5 s, when little braking remains. In between it correctly finds that no catch exists, but 13 of its 33 aborts come down near the pad: braking late, it is lower and faster than the baseline at the same moment (72 m lower at 6 s) and has less time to steer clear. The baseline brakes earlier, so its aborts usually clear the pad, but it rarely saves the booster. The engine-out reserve survives a failure at any time except 1 and 2 s. It brakes harder while 13 engines are available and descends more slowly on three, landing with 0.5 t instead of 5.5 t.

Crosswind

Both controllers fail between 20 and 25 m/s of mean wind, the MPC later than the baseline. At 25 m/s the wind pushes the 71 m booster sideways at about 0.6 m/s² plus gusts, more than the terminal descent's 2.7° tilt can correct. That limit comes from the catch geometry, not from either guidance law.

Capture basin

The ignition point was shifted by up to ±200 m towards or away from the tower and ±300 m in altitude, giving 143 points in calm air. The MPC catches 127 with no pad strikes; the baseline catches 86 with 25 pad strikes. The fixed braking profile cannot absorb a low or far-offshore start, and only discovers the problem at the gate, where an abort is too late to clear the pad. The MPC re-plans from wherever it is, and where no catch exists its planner reports this seconds earlier.

The cost of engine-out tolerance

The same 40 dispersed flights, 20 of them with an engine failure at a random time, were flown with 45, 50 and 55 t of landing propellant. About 5 t more (+11%) makes single-engine-out tolerance robust to the dispersions: 19 of 20 failures survived with no pad strikes (95% interval roughly 76–99%). Extra propellant alone does nothing for the tuned MPC, which catches 4 of 20 at every level. The braking-reserve constraint is what turns propellant into engine-out safety, by spending it on braking while 13 engines are available.

What broke along the way

Each of these was a failure in an earlier version, found by tracing individual flights. They are properties of the problem rather than incidental bugs, and probably the most transferable part of the work.

  1. Fuel-optimal plans ride every limit at once. The optimum brakes as late as possible at full thrust and full tilt. A thrust margin alone left the plan on the tilt limit, where any lateral error made it infeasible; it needed margins on both (8%, 2°).
  2. Actuator lag fights bang-bang plans. The engines' 0.25 s response to a max–min–max thrust plan lost 1–3 m/s of braking at every switch, always in the dangerous direction. Commanding the plan one lag early, modelling the engine-switch tail-off and entering the terminal descent one lag early removed the loss.
  3. The throttle floor makes over-braking a trap. Clipping an oversized lateral command by rotating the thrust vector turned the excess into braking and stalled the booster 12 m above the arms. The lateral component has to be trimmed instead.
  4. The estimator must give the planner what it needs. Explaining a thrust shortfall as extra mass reproduces the accelerations but corrupts the planner's propellant budget by about 10 t, causing false “catch impossible” decisions. The planner now receives engine thrust scaled by the estimated effectiveness.
  5. A planner must agree with itself. Re-planning from a point on its own plan, under the same rules, came back infeasible: drag entered in successive passes and the first pass ignored it, although drag supplies up to 8 m/s² of braking. This one consistency check removed most of the closed-loop propellant loss (about 2 t) and most false aborts.
  6. Constraints checked at grid nodes leak between them. Time-based rules checked only at grid points let plans cross between points. The next plan, on a different grid, found the same timing infeasible and slipped its arrival, costing about 0.5 t per half second. A node on every rule boundary fixed it.
  7. Infeasibility is a useful signal only if the model is honest. Aborting when the planner finds no solution decides seconds earlier than a heuristic could, but every modelling gap above first showed up as a false abort. The plan must also budget propellant for the phase it does not plan, the terminal descent, or it will succeed on paper and run dry over the arms.

Limitations

  • Point mass. The thrust direction stands in for attitude: there are no rotational dynamics, gimbal limits, grid fins, slosh or aerodynamic moments. The tower check uses the centre of mass plus the booster's radius, consistent with a body held near upright while the engines gimbal. The replay draws the body leaning about a third of the way into the thrust, with the plumes deflected for the rest; this is a rendering choice, not simulated attitude.
  • Public estimates. Masses, propellant, drag coefficients, tower geometry and tolerances are estimates. Absolute catch rates are not predictions for the real vehicle; the comparisons, mechanisms and trade-offs are the result.
  • Environment. Constant air density and a uniform mean wind with gusts; no wind shear or tower wake.
  • Engines. One total thrust with first-order lag. A real engine failure adds a thrust transient and a moment, and detection here is instantaneous.
  • Computation. 0.1–0.3 s per plan in Python; flight software would use an embedded solver.
  • Sample sizes. 40 flights per tuning setting and 12 per wind speed, so intervals are wide. They are reported throughout.

Conclusion

In this model, convex fuel-optimal guidance beats a tuned braking heuristic on every nominal measure of a tower catch: catch rate, precision, propellant margin, crosswind tolerance and capture basin. The same property that saves propellant, braking late, leaves less room to abort after a mid-burn engine failure. A 10 m/s approach cap largely restores that room at no cost in catch rate. Single-engine-out tolerance costs one more convex constraint and about 5 t of landing propellant. The throttle floor, rather than the guidance law, shapes the final seconds of the catch.
