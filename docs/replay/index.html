What 300 dispersed catches per controller showed

Every controller flew the same 300 flights: wind up to 15 m/s with gusts, drag ±20%, thrust and mass errors, sensor noise, a quarter-second engine lag, and in 56 flights an engine failure at a random moment. Settings were chosen beforehand on a separate tuning set.

Things the problem taught me

  • The throttle floor. Three Raptors at 40% almost exactly hover a near-empty booster, so it can brake hard but barely speed up its descent. Over-braking near the arms is a trap, and the final descent has to decelerate gently instead of coasting.
  • Optimal means on the edge. A fuel-optimal plan brakes as late as possible at full thrust and full tilt. It needs margins on both, or any small error makes it infeasible.
  • A quarter-second matters. Engine lag against abrupt optimal thrust switches lost 1-3 m/s of braking at every switch until the plan was commanded one lag early.
  • A planner must agree with itself. Re-planning from a point on its own plan said "impossible": drag was added in a second pass, and the drag-free first pass failed. Fixing that one inconsistency recovered about 2 t of propellant per flight.
  • Budget what you don't plan. The plan ended at a gate 12 m above the arms, so it never reserved fuel for the last 12 m; marginal flights "succeeded" and ran dry over the pad until the plan had to arrive with that fuel on board.

Simulation, not SpaceX data. Vehicle numbers are public estimates (Raptor 2: 2.26 MN, 40–100% throttle; Super Heavy empty ~275 t); the booster is a point mass whose thrust direction stands in for its attitude. Engines: 13 at ignition, the 3 centre engines from 3 s. The world adds drag, gusty wind, thrust and mass errors, sensor noise and engine failures.

Catch tolerance: within 1 m of the catch point, under 1.5 m/s down, under 0.5 m/s sideways, tilt under 3°. A booster that cannot be caught diverts offshore; landing more than 40 m from the tower counts as a safe miss.
